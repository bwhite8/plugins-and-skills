# Writing

Write content in a clear, substantive style inspired by Ethan Mollick and Andrew Ng. Prioritize insight over polish. Sound like an educated thought leader, not a short-form content creator.

## Voice Characteristics

**From Ethan Mollick:**
- Lead with observation and evidence, not assertion
- Use personal experience ("I found that...", "In my experiments...")
- Explore implications rather than declare conclusions
- Balance enthusiasm with intellectual honesty about limitations
- Reference research and cite specific findings
- Ask genuine questions that you then investigate

**From Andrew Ng:**
- Explain complex ideas through concrete examples
- Build understanding incrementally, one concept at a time
- Use analogies that connect to familiar experiences
- Maintain an encouraging, teacher-like tone without being condescending
- Focus on practical application and "how to actually do this"
- Keep technical accuracy while remaining accessible

## Sentence Rhythm and Development

Write like an essayist, not a copywriter. Ideas need room to breathe and develop.

### Avoid Excessive Brevity

Choppy, staccato sentences read like Twitter threads or LinkedIn posts. They signal low effort and shallow thinking.

**Bad (too choppy):**
> I had predictions. Age, technical background, job function. I thought these would map to adoption curves. They didn't.

**Better (developed):**
> I entered this role with assumptions about who would embrace AI quickly and who would push back. I expected certain demographics, technical backgrounds, and job functions to map cleanly onto adoption curves. Those predictions proved remarkably unreliable.

### Let Ideas Unfold Across Clauses

Complex thoughts deserve complex sentences. Use subordinate clauses, em-dashes, and semicolons to develop ideas fully within a single sentence when appropriate.

**Underdeveloped:**
> Skeptics rarely convert from demos. They convert from peer success.

**Developed:**
> Skeptics rarely convert based on a single conversation or demonstration, no matter how compelling. They tend to shift their views after watching peers they trust succeed with the technology over time.

### Balance Short and Long

Short sentences can punctuate and emphasize. But they shouldn't dominate. A piece composed mostly of short sentences reads breathless and unsophisticated. Aim for a natural rhythm where longer, more developed sentences carry the weight of your ideas, with shorter sentences providing occasional emphasis or transition.

**Good rhythm example:**
> That confidence eroded quickly in the best possible way. An operations analyst showed me a prompting technique I had never encountered despite months of daily use. A customer service lead discovered a use case that had never occurred to me. A finance manager developed a workflow that was more elegant and effective than anything I had designed on my own.

Notice: One short sentence for emphasis, followed by three developed sentences that carry the actual content.

- "And honestly?"
- "Let me be clear..."
- "Here's the thing..."
- "The reality is..."
- "At the end of the day..."

## Sentence Patterns to AVOID

### The "Not X, it's Y" Construction
**Bad:** "It's not about working harder, it's about working smarter."
**Better:** "Working smarter matters more than working harder."
**Best:** Just make your point directly without the theatrical setup.

### The False Binary
**Bad:** "You can either embrace AI or be left behind."
**Better:** Acknowledge nuance. Most choices aren't binary.

### The Rhetorical Question Opener
**Bad:** "What if I told you there was a better way?"
**Better:** Just tell them. Skip the infomercial setup.

### The Question-Then-Answer Pattern
**Bad:** "What did predict adoption? Curiosity, mostly."
**Better:** "Curiosity emerged as a far better predictor of adoption than any demographic variable."

This pattern feels clever but reads as gimmicky. State findings directly.

### The Cliffhanger Transition
**Bad:** "But that's not all. There's something even more important."
**Better:** Just say the important thing.

### Overly Parallel Structure
**Bad:** "AI can write. AI can code. AI can create. But can AI think?"
**Better:** Vary sentence length and structure naturally.

### The Obvious Observation
**Bad:** "AI is changing everything. And that means we need to adapt."
**Better:** Be specific about what's changing and how.

### Hollow Superlatives
**Bad:** "This is incredibly important for absolutely everyone."
**Better:** Say why it matters and to whom specifically.

## Structural Patterns to AVOID

### Every Paragraph Same Length
Vary your paragraphs. Some should be one sentence. Others can be longer explorations. Monotonous rhythm is the enemy of engagement.

### Listicles Without Depth
Don't just list things. If you're listing, add genuine insight to each point. Better yet, weave ideas into prose.

### The Five-Paragraph Essay
Introduction, three points, conclusion. This structure screams "assignment." Let your structure follow your argument.

### Excessive Hedging
**Bad:** "It might be possible that this could potentially help some people in certain situations."
**Better:** "This helps when [specific condition]."

### Front-Loading Qualifications
**Bad:** "While it's true that there are many factors to consider, and different people may have different needs, generally speaking..."
**Better:** Make your point. Add nuance after.

## What TO Do Instead

### Be Specific
**Weak:** "AI is getting better at writing."
**Strong:** "GPT-4 can now pass the bar exam. A year ago, it couldn't."

### Show Your Thinking
**Weak:** "This approach works best."
**Strong:** "I tested three approaches. The first failed because X. The second worked but took too long. The third hit the sweet spot."

### Use Real Examples
Don't say "imagine a scenario where..." Just describe an actual scenario you've seen or experienced.

### Vary Sentence Length (But Favor Development)
Short sentences punch. Longer sentences can explore an idea more fully, allowing you to develop a thought across multiple clauses and create a different rhythm in the reader's mind. Mix them — but let developed sentences dominate. A piece that's mostly short sentences reads like a content mill. See "Sentence Rhythm and Development" above for detailed guidance.

### Let Paragraphs Breathe
One idea per paragraph. If you're cramming multiple ideas together, split them.

### Trust the Reader
Don't explain what you just said. Don't summarize before you've made your point. Don't tell them why they should care—show them something interesting and let them decide.

### Be Willing to Be Wrong
Mollick regularly says "I don't know" and "I was surprised to find." This builds trust. Don't pretend certainty you don't have.

## Before Publishing Checklist

1. Read it aloud. Does it sound like a person talking — an educated person, not a content creator?
2. Search for every word in the "never use" list
3. Look for banned patterns: "not X, it's Y", question-then-answer, rhetorical questions
4. Check sentence rhythm: Are most sentences developed, with short ones used sparingly for emphasis?
5. Check: Does every paragraph earn its place?
6. Ask: What would I cut if I had to cut 20%?
7. Final pass: Remove remaining filler words (very, really, just, actually, basically)

## Example Transformations

### Corporate AI-Speak (Avoid)
> In today's rapidly evolving landscape, it's more important than ever to leverage AI tools effectively. But here's the truth: it's not about the technology itself, it's about how we utilize it. By fostering a culture of innovation and taking a holistic approach, organizations can navigate this transformative period and create truly impactful solutions.

**Problems:** Banned words everywhere. "Not X, it's Y" construction. Empty modifiers. No actual insight.

### Choppy Content-Creator Style (Also Avoid)
> I had assumptions. About who would adopt AI. About who would resist. I was wrong. Age didn't matter. Background didn't matter. What mattered? Curiosity. That's it. Curiosity and a tolerance for feeling clumsy.

**Problems:** Reads like a LinkedIn post. Fragments pile up. Question-then-answer gimmick. No developed thoughts. Sounds breathless and unsophisticated.

### Mollick/Ng Style (Target)
> I entered this role with assumptions about who would embrace AI quickly and who would push back. I expected certain demographics, technical backgrounds, and job functions to map cleanly onto adoption curves. Those predictions proved remarkably unreliable. Curiosity emerged as a far better predictor than any demographic variable, along with a willingness to tolerate feeling clumsy and incompetent during the learning process.

**Why it works:** Specific observations. Developed sentences that give ideas room to unfold. Natural rhythm. Personal but substantive. An actual insight the reader can use.
