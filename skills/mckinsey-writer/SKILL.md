# McKinsey Writer

## Overview
This style mimics a research-informed, consultative business writing approach that balances analytical rigor with accessible insights. The tone is authoritative yet conversational, grounded in data while remaining forward-looking.

## Core Characteristics

### Tone
- **Professional but accessible**: Avoid jargon-heavy language; explain concepts clearly
- **Evidence-based**: Ground claims in research, data, or observable trends
- **Balanced**: Present multiple perspectives before synthesizing insights
- **Consultative**: Position as a trusted advisor, not prescriptive authority
- **Measured optimism**: Acknowledge challenges while highlighting opportunities

### Voice Elements
- Use first-person plural sparingly ("In my experience")
- Employ hedging language to maintain credibility: "tend to," "can," "might," "often"
- Reference quantitative data naturally: "fewer than 20 percent," "a plurality," "about a third"
- Acknowledge uncertainty: "not yet clear," "still emerging," "early indicators suggest"

## Sentence Structure Patterns

### Opening Sentences
Start paragraphs by establishing context or common understanding:
- "As organizations expand their use of [topic]..."
- "While many see [common approach], focusing only on [limitation]..."
- "Looking at [area], [observation]..."
- "In addition to [prerequisite], [additional factor]..."

### Building Complexity
Use compound sentences with commas to layer ideas:
```
"Positioning AI as an enabler of growth and innovation creates space within the organization to go after the cost and efficiency improvements more effectively."
```

### Transitional Phrases
Connect ideas smoothly:
- "In addition to..."
- "For example,"
- "Still, even in these early days..."
- "This is another one of..."
- "Looking at..."

### Comparative Structures
Highlight differences and similarities:
- "[High performers] are more likely than others to..."
- "Organizations that [action] are the same ones that [outcome]"
- "In most [category], fewer than [percentage]..."

## Paragraph Construction

### Typical Flow
1. **Context/Setup**: Establish the current state or common approach
2. **Contrast/Complication**: Introduce nuance, limitation, or alternative perspective
3. **Evidence**: Support with data, research, or specific examples
4. **Insight/Implication**: Conclude with forward-looking synthesis

### Example Structure
```
[Context] "Often, organizations approach AI through a cost-first mindset."
[Contrast] "While many see leading indicators from efficiency gains, focusing only on cost can limit AI's impact."
[Insight] "Positioning AI as an enabler of growth and innovation creates space within the organization to go after the cost and efficiency improvements more effectively."
[Implication] "And for many organizations, an efficiency play will not be sufficient to navigate AI disruption."
```

## Language Patterns

### Qualifying Statements
- "not yet clear"
- "still in [early phase]"
- "tend to"
- "can" (possibility, not certainty)
- "might affect"

### Comparative Language
- "high performers are more likely than others"
- "increasing demand for"
- "larger companies in particular"
- "twice as likely to"

### Observational Language
- "we are seeing"
- "respondents share differing perspectives"
- "a plurality of respondents observed"
- "interestingly"

### Research References
- "In my experience,"
- "broader [Research Name] research"
- "based on more than [X] [studies/transformations]"
- "(Exhibit [#])"
- "experts tested to determine"

## Common Rhetorical Moves

### Setting Up Contrasts
Present conventional wisdom, then offer a more nuanced view:
- "While [common approach], [limitation or alternative]"
- "Many see [surface benefit], but [deeper consideration]"

### Building from Specifics to Principles
- Cite specific examples or data points
- Connect to broader patterns
- Link to strategic implications

### Acknowledging Complexity
- Present multiple perspectives
- Note exceptions or variations
- Avoid overly prescriptive statements

### Forward-Looking Synthesis
End paragraphs or sections by pointing toward implications:
- "They will need to consider how..."
- "Some of these jobs will become more critical as..."
- "These practices enable organizations to..."

## Paragraph Length
- **Typical**: 3-5 sentences
- **Opening paragraphs**: May run longer (5-7 sentences) to establish context
- **Transitional paragraphs**: Can be shorter (2-3 sentences)

## Example Applications

### When Introducing a Finding
"In addition to [prerequisite factor], [high-performing group] are also more likely to employ [practice]. For example, [specific instance]. This is [significance of finding]. The full set of [practices/findings] align with [broader framework], which is based on [credibility marker]."

### When Presenting Uncertainty
"As [situation continues], [stakeholders] share differing perspectives on [outcome]. Looking at [specific area], a plurality of [group] observed [finding]. In most [category], fewer than [percentage] report [outcome], and smaller shares say [alternative outcome]."

### When Connecting to Strategic Implications
"Doing so also supports [secondary benefit]. [Stakeholders] tend to rally behind [approach]. In my experience, many of the organizations that [action] are the same ones that [positive outcome]."

## Keywords and Phrases to Use
- "high performers"
- "at scale"
- "value attributable to"
- "essential to capturing value"
- "enable organizations to"
- "in the year ahead"
- "nature of work"
- "incorporating [X] into existing roles or workflows"
- "become more critical as"
- "data readiness"

## Avoid
- Absolute statements without hedging
- Prescriptive "you must" language
- Overly casual tone
- Unsubstantiated claims
- Dense jargon without context
- Short, choppy sentences without variation

---

**Use this style when**: Writing research summaries, consulting reports, thought leadership content, or strategic recommendations that need to balance authority with accessibility while maintaining analytical rigor.
